# Scene World Exploration

**Status:** Working design-discovery brief  
**Date:** July 26, 2026  
**Purpose:** Define what Scene means before prescribing a visual aesthetic

## Starting correction

“Your personal concert archive meets an aftermovie” was a useful early image, but it is too literal to serve as Scene’s full creative thesis.

The aftermovie can inspire moments of celebration and sharing. It should not dictate every screen or reduce Scene to festival imagery.

Scene needs a world that emerges from the psychology of the product:

- experiences become memories;
- memories become a collection;
- comparison creates clarity;
- the ordered collection reveals identity;
- friends make that identity social.

## The product beneath the features

### Functional job

Help me remember the live shows I have experienced and determine which ones mattered most.

### Emotional job

Help me understand my own history through the nights that stayed with me.

### Social job

Give me a compelling way to show my taste, debate it, and discover where it overlaps with the people I care about.

### Transformation

Before Scene:

> I have scattered photos, half-remembered dates, and a vague sense of my favorite shows.

After Scene:

> I can see the live-music life I have built, what sits at the top, why those nights stayed with me, and how my Scene compares with yours.

## The four product atoms

Every major product and design decision should reinforce at least one of these:

1. **Experience**  
   A specific artist, place, and date.

2. **Memory**  
   The personal residue of being there.

3. **Order**  
   Comparison turns a pile of memories into a meaningful personal canon.

4. **Connection**  
   Shared attendance and ranking differences create conversation between people.

## Emotional sequence

Scene should not maintain one emotional volume throughout the app.

| Moment | Intended feeling |
|---|---|
| Personal invitation | Chosen |
| Memory prompt | Curious |
| Searching for a show | Recognized |
| Saving a memory | Possessive |
| Growing the collection | Momentum |
| Comparing shows | Tension |
| Watching a rank settle | Clarity |
| Revealing a Top 10 | Pride |
| Comparing with a friend | Intimacy and debate |
| Sharing | Self-expression |

The everyday interface should provide clarity and calm. Ranking, milestones, and sharing earn the right to become expressive.

## Design tensions to preserve

Scene should be:

- emotional without becoming sentimental;
- premium without becoming precious;
- music-native without using genre clichés;
- club-literate without becoming club-themed;
- personal without feeling private or closed;
- social without becoming a feed;
- archival without feeling static;
- energetic without living at festival volume;
- visually distinctive without fighting the user’s photos;
- bold in celebration and quiet in collection.

## Beachhead: dance music

Scene should be capable of expanding across live music, but its first audience is people with taste in dance music. That is an advantage, not a costume brief.

The product should feel at home beside the visual culture of respected clubs, labels, promoters, and electronic artists: confident typography, strong image direction, selective color, cultural fluency, and very little explanation.

It should not rely on:

- generic neon rave gradients;
- lasers, equalizers, waveforms, or turntables as identity;
- festival wristbands, tickets, or venue ephemera as the primary metaphor;
- “EDM” visual clichés;
- constant maximalism.

The useful standard is:

> Club-literate, not club-themed.

The dance-music audience should recognize the taste level before they recognize a literal reference.

## Reference hierarchy

The strongest references play different roles. Scene should not visually clone any one of them.

### DICE: cultural credibility and restraint

DICE proves that a live-music product can feel fast, direct, and culturally fluent without coating every screen in concert imagery. Scene should study its hierarchy, confidence, economy, and ability to feel connected to the culture it serves.

### Airbuds: music identity as friendship

Airbuds makes music taste conversational, playful, and socially alive. Scene should study how personality enters profiles, how friends create the return loop, and how a recap becomes a lightweight piece of identity.

### Retro: memory becomes an artifact

Retro treats the external share as part of the product rather than a screenshot of the product. Scene should study its intimate framing, recap rhythm, image treatment, and ability to turn personal history into something people are proud to post.

### Beli: opinion becomes a collection

Beli remains the strongest behavioral reference for progressively building a ranked personal canon and using friends’ collections as an acquisition loop.

## Two related visual modes

Scene should have one design system with two levels of expression.

### Core UI

The app itself should be:

- controlled;
- fast;
- image-led;
- typographically confident;
- calm enough to use repeatedly;
- expressive only when the user earns a moment.

This is where DICE is the more useful reference.

### Broadcast UI

Anything made to leave the app should be:

- immediately legible in a 9:16 story;
- visually complete without explanatory copy;
- more expressive than the everyday interface;
- personalized enough to communicate the sharer’s identity;
- recognizably Scene even when the logo is small;
- designed around real artist and user imagery;
- built with a clear invitation or comparison hook.

This is where Retro and Airbuds are the more useful references.

The contrast is intentional: the app earns trust through restraint; the share earns attention through expression.

## Share is a primary canvas

Sharing is not a final export task. It is a product surface and a core growth mechanism.

Each meaningful milestone should be evaluated for its story artifact:

- first five shows;
- ranking unlocked;
- first number-one show;
- Top 10;
- a new show entering or upsetting the Top 10;
- shared attendance;
- Scene Match;
- venue, city, artist, or year patterns;
- a memorable prompt answer;
- an invitation to compare.

Every share format should answer:

1. What does this reveal about me?
2. Why would I be proud or amused enough to post it?
3. Can a viewer understand it in one second?
4. Is there a reason for a specific friend to respond?
5. Does the path back into Scene feel native and low-friction?

## What the current visual system communicates

The current system uses:

- near-black navy backgrounds;
- cyan, orange, yellow, and violet accents;
- blurred color orbs;
- glow effects;
- dark photo cards;
- system typography;
- rounded cards and glass-like surfaces;
- a spaced all-caps SCENE wordmark with a star.

### Worth preserving

- dark environments that make live photography feel luminous;
- photography as evidence of experience;
- ceremonial treatment of the number-one show;
- the star as a possible marker of significance;
- the willingness to let major moments feel dramatic.

### Ready to evolve

- “concert vibe” gradients are not an ownable idea;
- glow is currently used as decoration rather than meaning;
- glass cards make Scene resemble many other dark consumer apps;
- the palette has too many equal accents;
- system typography cannot carry the desired editorial identity alone;
- visual intensity is too constant, reducing the impact of milestone moments.

## Seven possible world seeds

These are meaning systems, not finished styles.

### 1. Afterimage

**Idea:** The show ends, but an image remains.

**Why it fits:** It connects memory, light, time, photography, and the residue of an experience without illustrating a concert literally.

**Possible behaviors:**

- light and color persist after interactions;
- ranked cards leave subtle traces as they move;
- memories sharpen from blur into focus;
- overlapping friends create blended afterimages;
- a reveal feels like an image developing.

**Risk:** It can collapse into generic neon, blur, or rave imagery if not disciplined.

### 2. Personal Canon

**Idea:** Scene helps you author the definitive edition of your own live-music history.

**Why it fits:** It makes ranking feel consequential and gives the collection cultural weight.

**Possible behaviors:**

- strong editorial hierarchy;
- numbered entries treated like chapters;
- restrained typography and confident negative space;
- Top 10 reveals resembling a published annual or cultural index;
- each update feels like revising the canon.

**Risk:** It can become cold, elitist, or overly magazine-like.

### 3. Resonance

**Idea:** Some nights continue vibrating through you, and friends resonate differently.

**Why it fits:** It naturally connects memory, ranking, and Scene Match.

**Possible behaviors:**

- tactile pulses and restrained haptics;
- layers align or interfere when comparing;
- ranking confidence appears as a signal resolving;
- connections are shown through rhythm and proximity rather than social-feed chrome.

**Risk:** Waveforms and audio-reactive graphics are familiar music-app clichés.

### 4. Living Archive

**Idea:** Scene is a beautiful archive that never stops reorganizing itself.

**Why it fits:** It supports collection, long-term trust, uncertain dates, personal artifacts, and an evolving rank.

**Possible behaviors:**

- archival labels and metadata;
- physical depth and considered organization;
- memories appear collected rather than posted;
- rankings feel like an evolving index;
- hidden shows feel stored, not deleted.

**Risk:** It can become static, beige, nostalgic, or museum-like.

### 5. Trace

**Idea:** Your Scene is the trace left by where music has taken you.

**Why it fits:** It connects venue, date, map, time, and social overlap.

**Possible behaviors:**

- shows become points along a personal path;
- cities and venues emerge gradually;
- friendships reveal intersecting traces;
- rank changes leave a readable history.

**Risk:** It may overemphasize geography and resemble a travel product.

### 6. Keepsake

**Idea:** Each show becomes an object worth keeping.

**Why it fits:** It gives memory prompts, photos, companions, and personal notes emotional weight.

**Possible behaviors:**

- layered personal artifacts;
- small signs of handling and ownership;
- show cards vary based on what the user contributes;
- sharing feels like handing someone an object.

**Risk:** It can become scrapbook-like, overly literal, or visually inconsistent.

### 7. Pulse

**Idea:** The archive is alive because taste keeps changing.

**Why it fits:** It supports ranking debt, ongoing additions, and dynamic social comparison.

**Possible behaviors:**

- rankings visibly settle;
- pending comparisons create controlled tension;
- new shows send a pulse through the collection;
- milestones expand the visual system’s energy.

**Risk:** It can feel like fitness tracking, nightlife promotion, or a generic event app.

## Recommended territories to prototype

The strongest options combine complementary seeds rather than choosing one literally.

### Territory A: Afterimage + Personal Canon

**Working thought:** What stays with you, put in order.

This territory combines sensory memory with strong editorial structure.

Everyday screens remain controlled and typographic. Photography and residual color provide emotion. Ranking and reveals can become cinematic because the underlying system is disciplined.

For the dance-music beachhead, this should inherit DICE’s restraint and cultural confidence without imitating its brand. The share layer can push much further: more graphic, more personal, and more kinetic than the core UI.

### Territory B: Living Archive + Resonance

**Working thought:** A collection that is still alive.

This territory treats memories as durable artifacts while making ranking and friendship feel dynamic.

It could be quieter and more timeless than the current brand, with motion and haptics carrying more emotion than decorative gradients.

### Territory C: Trace + Keepsake

**Working thought:** The nights, places, and people that made your Scene.

This territory centers personal history and shared attendance.

It could feel intimate, spatial, and highly personal, with the map and companions becoming meaningful without turning Scene into a social feed.

## Current recommendation

**Territory A, Afterimage + Personal Canon, is the strongest first hypothesis.**

This is not a recommendation for a neon visual style.

It is a recommendation for a design philosophy:

- the structure is clear, editorial, and enduring;
- the emotional layer appears as light, image, motion, and memory residue;
- ranking creates increasing clarity;
- milestone moments can become visually explosive without making the whole app exhausting;
- the resulting profile feels authored rather than populated.
- DICE informs restraint, Airbuds informs social identity, and Retro informs the share artifact;
- the core app and the broadcast layer feel related, but do not operate at the same visual volume.

The key phrase is:

> What stays with you, put in order.

## Mobbin research brief

Mobbin should be used to study product patterns, not to assemble a collage of fashionable screens.

### Flows to collect

1. Personalized invitation and deep-link onboarding
2. Guest-first creation before authentication
3. Prompt decks and progressive collection
4. Search and fast object creation
5. Collection milestones
6. Pairwise preference or ranking
7. Ranking-debt or unfinished-task presentation
8. Public/private profile controls
9. “I did this too” or shared-object import
10. Friend taste comparison
11. Annual or milestone reveal
12. Native sharing and invitation

### Products worth examining

- **DICE:** cultural fluency, confident hierarchy, live-music discovery, restraint
- **Beli:** ranked collection, map, friend comparison, adding from others
- **Letterboxd:** identity through a public collection, diary, favorites
- **Retro:** intimate friend network, photo memory, invitation, story-native artifacts
- **Airbuds:** social music identity, friend-first behavior, expressive recaps
- **Spotify:** personalized reveal, Blend, taste identity
- **Partiful:** personal invitation, low-friction entry, event identity
- **Cosmos:** premium visual collection and calm browsing
- **Strava:** personal archive becoming social identity
- **How We Feel / Stoic:** prompts that create reflection without feeling like forms
- **Duolingo:** visible progress, milestones, and return motivation
- **Apple Journal / Photos:** memory, privacy, native materiality

### What to record from each example

- What job is the screen doing?
- What is the emotional state before and after?
- What is required versus optional?
- Where does authentication occur?
- What makes the interaction feel native?
- What is visually distinctive versus merely fashionable?
- What should Scene borrow as a pattern?
- What should Scene explicitly avoid?

## Evaluation rubric for the three worlds

Each visual territory should be scored against:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Product truth | Does the world make memory, ranking, identity, and connection feel inevitable? |
| Ownability | Could this be recognized as Scene without the logo? |
| Cross-genre range | Does it work for house, punk, country, jazz, pop, and everything between? |
| Emotional range | Can it move from calm collection to explosive reveal? |
| Photo compatibility | Does it elevate unpredictable user and artist imagery? |
| Native quality | Does it feel designed for the hand, motion, haptics, and iPhone conventions? |
| Social value | Does it make comparison and sharing feel natural rather than bolted on? |
| Story value | Would a tasteful dance-music fan genuinely want to put the output on their Instagram Story? |
| Longevity | Can it survive beyond a current visual trend? |
| Buildability | Can the React and Capacitor system reproduce it reliably? |
| Accessibility | Does it remain legible and usable without depending on motion, blur, or color? |

## Design-lab deliverables

The first visual exploration should produce:

1. a one-page product-world statement for each territory;
2. a type, color, material, image, motion, and haptic grammar;
3. the same 8 to 10 core screens in each territory;
4. one key transition or reveal prototype per territory;
5. one public Top 10 share artifact per territory;
6. a scored evaluation using the rubric above;
7. Sam’s selected direction;
8. a Figma design system derived from the winner;
9. a coded iPhone design-lab prototype before production implementation.

## Open questions

- Is Scene primarily an authored canon, a living memory archive, or a social taste signal?
- Should the visual world feel more intimate or more declarative by default?
- How much evidence of physical experience should remain once the design becomes less literal?
- Does the star belong as a meaningful marker, or is it part of the current generic glow language?
- What visual behavior should be uniquely Scene’s equivalent of a signature interaction?
- What should a Scene profile communicate in the first three seconds?
